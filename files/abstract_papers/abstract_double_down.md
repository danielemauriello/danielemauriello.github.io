Should investors buy more of a stock that has fallen in value? Normatively, the answer should not depend on whether they already own it. Yet, using nearly three million transactions from a large brokerage, we show that investors' purchase decisions do depend on prior ownership. Over the preceding six months, stocks in which investors increased their positions underperformed stocks they purchased for the first time by 4 to 7.3 percentage points per month. A recent negative return raises the probability of buying an already-owned stock by about a quarter, while investors trade stocks they do not own on momentum. We test whether motivated reasoning drives this doubling down: investors may become optimistic about losing stocks they chose, to avoid realizing the loss and to protect their self-image. In a pre-registered two-session experiment, participants choose an asset, experience a loss, and are randomly assigned either to keep it or to have it automatically liquidated. Owners are no more optimistic than participants whose position was liquidated, and eliciting beliefs before the investment decision does not change the ownership effect. Owners double down slightly more often, but the difference is not statistically significant. Our results point to preferences or attention, rather than distorted beliefs, as the source of doubling down.
